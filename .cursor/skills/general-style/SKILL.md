# Writing Primitives

Write so the reader can follow one developing line of thought without reconstructing the argument themselves.

Clarity comes primarily from selecting, ordering, and relating ideas. Sentence polish is secondary.

## Core primitives

### 1. Establish the governing point

Know what the passage is trying to explain, establish, or enable.

For substantial writing, identify the following.

- The central question or purpose.
- The main conclusion, explanation, or takeaway.
- The few focal points required to reach it.

Do not give secondary observations equal structural weight to the main argument.

### 2. Build forward

Arrange ideas in dependency order.

Introduce a concept before relying on it. Establish context before consequences. Give evidence where the claim it supports is being developed.

Once a point has been established, move forward from it. Refer back when necessary, but do not repeatedly re-explain, reframe, or rediscover it.

### 3. Give each unit one job

A section should advance one stage of the overall narrative.

A paragraph should develop one focal point.

A sentence should contribute a distinct claim, explanation, relation, example, qualification, or piece of evidence.

Split units that are doing unrelated jobs. Merge units that separately repeat the same job.

### 4. Control the detail hierarchy

Prefer a few developed points over many lightly treated ones.

Include detail when it materially improves the following.

- Understanding.
- Evidence.
- Execution.
- Distinction between alternatives.
- Interpretation of the main point.

Otherwise omit it, subordinate it, or leave it to a more specific document or skill.

Do not mistake completeness for accumulation.

### 5. Qualify locally and once

State uncertainty, scope, assumptions, and exceptions where they affect the claim.

Make the qualification precise. Then continue under that established scope.

Do not repeatedly hedge later statements merely because an earlier limitation exists. Reintroduce a qualification only when its meaning changes or the reader could reasonably lose track of it.

### 6. Keep terminology stable

Use one term for one concept unless a distinction requires different language.

Do not rotate synonyms merely to avoid repetition. In technical and analytical prose, exact repetition is often clearer than lexical variety.

Define unfamiliar terms before using them as shorthand.

### 7. Prefer information over commentary about information

State the point rather than announcing that a point is coming.

Prefer the following.

`The cache is invalidated after each write.`

Avoid the following.

`An important point to consider is how cache invalidation works.`

Avoid throat-clearing, repeated previews, ceremonial transitions, significance inflation, and summaries that merely restate the preceding paragraph.

Signposting is useful when it reveals structure the reader could not otherwise infer.

### 8. Make relationships explicit

Do not leave important logical connections implicit.

When necessary, state whether a sentence does one of the following.

- Causes something.
- Supports something.
- Contrasts with something.
- Depends on something.
- Qualifies something.
- Exemplifies something.
- Follows from something.

Use transitions to express actual relationships, not to decorate the prose.

### 9. Prefer precise, direct language

Use the simplest wording that preserves the required technical meaning.

Prefer concrete nouns and verbs over abstract noun phrases. Prefer active constructions when the actor matters.

Remove modifiers, intensifiers, and evaluative language that do not add information.

Do not simplify away necessary technical distinctions.

### 10. Preserve substance and voice

Editing for clarity must not change facts, claims, notation, uncertainty, or technical meaning.

Do not invent specificity to make prose seem concrete.

When revising existing human writing, preserve intentional voice and irregularity unless they obstruct the reader.

Human writing does not require manufactured informality, quirks, errors, or artificial variation.

## Revision order

Revise from largest scale to smallest.

1. **Narrative.** Is there a clear governing point and direction?
2. **Sequence.** Does each idea appear when its prerequisites are available?
3. **Focus.** Are the important points developed and secondary material subordinated?
4. **Redundancy.** Does the text move forward rather than revisit settled material?
5. **Paragraphs.** Does each paragraph have one identifiable job?
6. **Sentences.** Is each sentence adding something distinct?
7. **Language.** Can the same meaning be expressed more directly?
8. **Accuracy.** Did revision preserve every necessary fact, qualification, and distinction?

Do not polish sentences that should first be moved, merged, or removed.

## Final check

Before delivering substantive writing, verify the following.

- A reader can state the main point without assembling it from scattered details.
- The sequence can be explained as a progression rather than a collection of topics.
- Major concepts are introduced once and subsequently built upon.
- Qualifications occur where needed without spreading hesitation through the rest of the text.
- Each paragraph has a clear reason to exist at its particular location.
- Secondary detail does not compete with the main line.
- Terminology remains stable.
- Repeated summaries, previews, and meta-commentary have been removed unless they provide genuine navigation.
- No sentence remains solely because it sounds polished.
- The text stops when its purpose has been fulfilled.

## Relationship to other skills

This skill governs baseline prose and information structure.

Domain-specific skills may define document forms, evidence standards, citation practices, notation, section conventions, examples, or audience-specific requirements.

Those skills should extend these primitives rather than duplicate them.
