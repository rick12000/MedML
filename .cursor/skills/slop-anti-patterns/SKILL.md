# Slop anti-patterns

Apply when the deliverable includes prose for the user: answers, summaries, docs, reports, or edited copy. Use alongside `general-style` for structure and line of thought.

Do not apply to reasoning traces, status-only updates, internal summaries, or code (including comments treated as code edits).

Keep the core content. Cut only the packaging below. Keep the facts, claims, names, numbers, and the writer's point.

## Words

Cut these outright:

delve, foster, leverage, utilize, facilitate, empower, streamline, robust, cutting-edge, paradigm shift, game changer, this is huge, this changes everything, tapestry, realm, beacon, multifaceted, meticulous, intricate, paramount, transformative, elevate, embark, supercharge, harness, ever-evolving.

## Adverbs

Cut these when they add nothing:

just, literally, honestly, simply, actually, truly, fundamentally, importantly, crucially, inherently, inevitably.

Keep one when it carries emphasis, uncertainty, contrast, or the writer's natural spoken rhythm.

## Phrases

Cut these when they delay the point:

it's worth noting, it's important to note, at the end of the day, when it comes to, at its core, in today's world, in the age of, in the world of, the reality is, the truth is, in terms of, with regard to, in order to, going forward, in this article, let's dive in.

Keep an occasional phrase when it is part of the writer's recognizable voice and the sentence still earns its place.

## Patterns

Cut each pattern below. State the point in a plain sentence.

1. **Binary contrasts.** Cut "This is not X. It's Y.", "The question isn't X, it's Y.", and "It's not just X but Y." State the point directly. "The question isn't the model. It's the eval." becomes "The eval matters more than the model."

2. **Throat-clearing openers.** Cut "Here's the thing,", "Here's what I mean,", "Let me be clear,", "I'll be honest,", and "The uncomfortable truth is." State the point.

3. **Faux-insight setups.** Cut "This is the part most people skip,", "What most people get wrong,", "Here's what nobody tells you,", and "The part everyone misses." State the claim on its own. "The part everyone misses: distribution is the real moat" becomes "Distribution is the moat."

4. **Colon reveals.** Cut a noun phrase, a colon, and a lowercase dramatic reveal: "The detail that makes it work: a separate agent grades it." "The best part: it learns." Write a plain sentence: "A separate agent does the grading, which is what makes it work." Use colons for lists, labels, and quotes. Prefer sentence case after a colon unless grammar, a proper noun, a title, or code requires otherwise.

5. **Superficial analysis.** Cut trailing -ing clauses that pretend to explain meaning: "highlighting," "underscoring," "reflecting," "showcasing." "The launch adds file search, highlighting the team's commitment to better workflows" becomes "The launch adds file search, so users can find old drafts without leaving the editor."

6. **Importance puffery.** Cut "stands as a testament," "marks a pivotal moment," "plays a vital role," "solidifies its position," and "underscores its significance." State the fact. "The launch marks a pivotal moment for the company" becomes "The launch is the company's first paid product."

7. **Interpretive metadiscourse.** Cut lines that tell the reader what to notice, how much weight to give it, or how to interpret the prose: "That last part matters more than it sounds,", "The key point is,", "As you can see,", "This distinction matters,", and a redundant "In other words." Delete the aside when the point is already clear. Replace it with support or facts already in the content when the point is not clear.

8. **Weasel attribution.** Cut "Experts agree,", "industry reports suggest,", "many argue,", "widely regarded as,", and "studies show." Name the source or cut the claim. Ask for a source when the user has none. Do not invent one.

9. **Fake-strong verbs.** Prefer "is" and "has" when they are clearer. "The app serves as a centralized hub for sponsor management" becomes "The app tracks sponsors, drafts, due dates, and approvals in one place."

10. **Synonym cycling.** Repeat the clear word. Cut rotated terms used only for style. "The agent reviews the draft. The assistant scores the piece. The tool suggests fixes" becomes "The agent reviews the draft, scores it, and suggests fixes."

11. **Negative listing.** Cut "Not a X. Not a Y. A Z." State Z.

12. **Dramatic fragmentation.** Cut "X. And Y. And Z." and "That's it. That's the whole thing." Use complete sentences.

13. **Robotic rhythm.** Cut repeated sentence shapes, identical paragraph structures, and stacked punchy fragments. Vary the shape only when it helps the point.

14. **Rhetorical setups.** Cut "What if I told you...", "Think about it:", "Plot twist:", and self-answered "Question? Answer." pairs. State the point.

15. **Fake-profound kickers.** Cut the final line when it turns the point into a metaphor, aphorism, or mic-drop sentence. Delete it. End on the clearest concrete sentence already in the draft. Add a plain takeaway or next action when the ending needs more closure.

16. **Summary-recap endings.** Cut "In conclusion,", "Ultimately,", "Overall,", and a final paragraph that restates the piece. End on the last concrete point, takeaway, or next action.

17. **Formatting slop.** Cut emoji in headings, bold sprinkled mid-sentence for emphasis, bullet lists where two sentences of prose would read better, and headers over two-sentence sections. Format should follow the content.

18. **Em dashes.** Cut em dashes used as a default rhythm. Use none in short copy. Keep 1–2 in a longer draft only when they beat a comma, a period, or parentheses. Remove clusters and decorative dashes.
